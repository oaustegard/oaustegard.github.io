Phantom Bass

Your ear hears a bass note even when the note's own frequency is missing. Three experiments show how, and how small speakers use it.

Start quiet. The volume slider at the bottom starts low and every tone fades in and out. Use headphones or proper speakers at a moderate level. A phone speaker hides the low notes, which is the point of part 3.

1. The missing fundamental

A pitched sound is a stack of partials at whole multiples of one frequency, the fundamental. Remove the fundamental and the pitch stays where it was: 200, 300 and 400 Hz together sound like 100 Hz, though nothing is playing at 100 Hz.

Why it works

The partials all repeat together once per period of the fundamental, so the waveform keeps the fundamental's period (the lower plot) with or without energy at that frequency. The auditory system tracks that periodicity. Schouten showed in 1940 that this residue pitch is not a distortion tone made in the ear: shift every partial by the same amount (part 2) and the pitch moves, while a difference tone could not.

2. Shift every partial

Now slide all three partials up or down by the same number of hertz. They stay 100 Hz apart, so their arithmetic difference never changes. The pitch you hear moves anyway, by about a third of the shift.

The compare buttons play the shifted sound, then a normal tone with its fundamental, twice over. Most listeners hear the predicted pitch as the closer match, though people differ: some hear the missing fundamental strongly, some follow the individual partials.

How the prediction is made

The ear behaves roughly as if it looks for the harmonic series that best fits the partials. With partials at 2×100+Δ, 3×100+Δ and 4×100+Δ, the best-fitting fundamental by least squares is 100 + Δ·(2+3+4)/(4+9+16), about 100 + 0.31Δ. That is close to de Boer's rule, the middle partial divided by its harmonic number (320/3 = 106.7 Hz for a shift of +20). For higher partials such as 5, 6, 7 the pitch moves less per hertz of shift. Near ±50 Hz the partials sit halfway between two series and the pitch becomes ambiguous.

3. Bass on a small speaker

A laptop or phone speaker can barely move air below about 150–200 Hz, and this bassline sits between 49 and 82 Hz. A virtual bass system removes the low fundamental and adds its harmonics instead, which the small speaker can play and your ear turns back into the low note.

Speaker

Small speaker runs everything through a steep filter below 180 Hz, so on headphones you hear roughly what a laptop plays. On a phone speaker, use Full range and let the real speaker do the cutting. Harmonics only plays partials 2 to 7 with the fundamental left out. Waveshaper is how most virtual bass plugins do it: the low band of the real bass is bent through a nonlinear curve that creates harmonics, and those replace it. All three carry about the same power before the speaker; through the small speaker the virtual versions come out several decibels louder in the bass range.
