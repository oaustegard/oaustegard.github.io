Embedding inversion

1,000 short strings were embedded with bekko-embedding-v1-a8m and a small inverter tried to write each one back from its 384-dim vector alone, then from the 384-bit sign code a 1-bit index keeps.         What this is

        An inverter in the shape of vec2text (Morris et al., 2023): a t5-small decoder conditioned on the target vector writes a first guess, then a second model reads the target, the guess, the guess's own embedding and the difference between them, and writes a better guess. Each round the encoder re-embeds the candidates and keeps whichever lands closest to the target. Five rounds. The nearest training string row in each arm is the control: the closest of the 40,000 training strings by the same cosine, which is what memorisation alone would return.

        The training budget was small on purpose: 40,000 pairs of questions and Wikipedia sentences, three epochs plus one for the corrector, 7.4 hours on four CPU cores. vec2text reports 92% exact recovery on 32-token inputs after five million pairs on a GPU. This run recovers the exact string 2.4% of the time from the float vector and 0.9% from the sign code, never past ten words, and lands on topic far more often than that. The outputs are not training strings; the float arm's guesses sit 0.09 cosine closer to the target than any training string does.

        Two rulers

        The float arm's cosine is between float vectors. The bin1 arm's is between two ±1 sign vectors, which equals 1 − 2 × the fraction of disagreeing bits, so 0.385 means 69% of bits agree. To put both arms on one scale the final strings from both were re-embedded in float space: bin1 0.529, float 0.635, against 0.546 and 0.515 for the two nearest-training-string controls. And scored with the sign code, the float arm's strings agree with the target on 71.8% of bits against 69.2% for the arm that was trained on the code.
